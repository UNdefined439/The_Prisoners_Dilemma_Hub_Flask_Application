The Setup

Two suspects are arrested and interrogated in separate rooms, with no way to talk to each other or agree on a story. Each one has to decide, entirely on their own, what to tell the police. What happens to them depends not just on their own choice, but on what the other person decides too — and neither will find out the other's choice until it's too late to change their own.

Cooperate or Defect

Each suspect has exactly two options: stay quiet and back the other person up, or turn on them and confess. In the language of the game, staying quiet is called cooperating [choosing the option that benefits both players, or the group, over yourself alone], and confessing is called defecting [choosing the option that benefits you individually, even at the other player's expense]. Every version of the Prisoner's Dilemma comes down to this same choice, made by both people at once.

The Payoff Matrix

What happens to each suspect depends on the combination of both choices, not just their own — this is usually mapped out in a payoff matrix [a grid showing the result of every possible combination of choices]. For our two suspects, it looks like this table. Mutual cooperation gets a light sentence for both. Mutual defection gets a harsher one for both. But when only one person defects, that person walks away completely free — at the other's expense.

The Dominant Strategy

Now imagine deciding what to do without knowing what the other suspect will pick. If they cooperate, you come out ahead by defecting — you go free instead of serving a year. If they defect, you still come out ahead by defecting — two years beats three. Either way, no matter what the other person chooses, defecting gives you the better result. That makes defecting a dominant strategy [a choice that gives you a better outcome than the alternative, no matter what the other player does] here.

The Dilemma

Here's the catch: if both suspects follow that same logic, they both defect — and both end up with two years. But if they'd both cooperated instead, they'd have gotten just one year each. Neither one can improve their own outcome by switching their choice alone, which is what makes mutual defection a Nash equilibrium [a combination of choices where no player can get a better result by changing only their own choice] — and yet it's worse for both of them than mutual cooperation would have been. That gap, between what's rational for each person individually and what's best for the two of them together, is the dilemma.

A Known Number of Rounds

Everything so far has assumed the suspects only play once. Now imagine the same two people play the game repeatedly — say, ten rounds — and both know that number going in. You might expect that to encourage cooperation. It doesn't. In the final round, there's no future left to protect, so defecting is once again the better move, exactly like a single round. But since both players know the last round ends in defection anyway, the second-to-last round has nothing left to protect either, so it collapses too — and that logic keeps working backward, round by round, all the way to the first move. This is called backward induction [reasoning about the final round first, then working back through each earlier round to see what it implies]. Knowing exactly how many rounds you'll play, it turns out, doesn't rescue cooperation at all.

An Unknown Number of Rounds

Take away that certainty, though, and the picture changes. Suppose neither player knows exactly when the game will end — maybe after every round there's just some chance it continues, and some chance it stops for good. Now there's no final round to reason backward from, since every round might be the last one, but also might not be. That uncertainty means a defection today could trigger round after round of retaliation tomorrow — a risk sometimes called the shadow of the future [the way possible future consequences shape what's rational to do right now]. Under the right conditions, that risk is enough to make cooperating the smarter long-term bet after all, which is a big part of why cooperation shows up far more often in ongoing relationships than the single-round logic would ever predict.

Tit for Tat

So if repetition can sustain cooperation, what's actually a good way to play? The most famous answer is tit for tat [start by cooperating, then on every later round just copy whatever the other player did last time]. It rewards cooperation with cooperation, punishes defection with defection, and forgives immediately the moment the other player goes back to cooperating. When researchers have pitted different repeated-play strategies against each other in tournaments, tit for tat has repeatedly come out on top — not because it's clever or complicated, but because it manages to be cooperative and hard to exploit at the same time. It isn't unbeatable, and how well any strategy performs still depends heavily on who else is playing, but for a simple, general-purpose approach, it's the one most often held up as the benchmark.

Other Strategies

Tit for tat is far from the only approach that's been tried. Researchers have tested countless repeated-play strategies over the years — more forgiving ones, more punishing ones, ones that adapt to how the other player has behaved so far, ones built to exploit patterns rather than reciprocate them — and no single strategy wins in every situation; how well one performs depends heavily on what it's up against. That variety is really the throughline of ongoing research into repeated play: not one correct way to play the game, but a whole landscape of strategies, each with its own strengths and blind spots.
