The Dilemma

Imagine two people who'd both be better off working together — but each one, thinking only of their own interest, comes out ahead by working against the other instead, no matter what the other person chooses to do. That's the Prisoner's Dilemma in a nutshell: a situation where the smartest move for each person individually adds up to a worse result for both of them than if they'd simply cooperated.

Where It Shows Up

The same tension shows up everywhere once you know the shape of it. Two countries stockpiling weapons instead of disarming, because neither trusts the other to go first. Companies locked in a price war, each afraid that backing off first just hands the other their customers. Roommates letting the dishes pile up, since doing them alone feels like a loss even though a clean kitchen is better for everyone. In each case, the individually rational move — arm up, keep cutting prices, leave the dishes — adds up to a worse outcome than if everyone had simply trusted each other enough to cooperate. That's the real reach of the Prisoner's Dilemma: less a story about two suspects, more a pattern for any situation where self-interest pulls against the group's best interest.

Where It Came From

The idea dates back to 1950, devised by mathematicians Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at the RAND Corporation, an influential Cold War-era think tank that studied game theory as part of its broader work on nuclear strategy and deterrence. It was mathematician Albert Tucker who shortly after gave the game its now-famous prisoner framing, turning an abstract payoff structure into the vivid, easy-to-follow scenario used ever since. That framing is a big part of why the idea has stuck around for more than 70 years as one of the most widely taught examples in game theory.
